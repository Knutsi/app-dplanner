How comparisons over time work

What the Time tab compares when it says a plan has moved, where those numbers come from, and where they can mislead. Every figure here is drawn by the prototype's own code, from one small example week.

The short version

DPlanner does not keep old versions of your plan. What it keeps is a daily summary of what the plan promised. Each summary says, for each milestone, how much work there was, how much of it was done, and when the milestone was due to start and land.

A summary is written automatically on a day the plan changed, and you can save one by hand under a name.

Every comparison on the Time tab is between two of these summaries: a then and a now. You pick both in the strip, and now is normally the live plan. The plots then show three things: how the promise moved, how much work there is, and how far the work has come against what the plan promises today.

The example week

One person works at full focus, starting Monday 7 September. The plan has two milestones:

  • v1 is Design (1 day) and then Build (2 days).
  • v2 is Polish (3 days) and Docs (2 days).

The week goes like this:

  • Monday: the plan is reviewed and saved as Kickoff review.
  • Tuesday: Design gets done, but nobody opens DPlanner, so no summary is written that day.
  • Wednesday: Build gets done, and v1 lands.
  • Thursday: a two-day step, Fix the import, is added to v2.
  • Friday (today): Polish is under way.

1. What gets written down, and when

There are two kinds of summary:

  • An automatic record is written on a day something in the plan changed: a step or an estimate is added or removed, a link, a status, or a milestone's date. It is written only when DPlanner is there to see the change, which means the window is open or someone runs dplanner progress record.
    • There is at most one record a day, and the last change of the day wins.
    • A day on which nothing changed gets no record.
  • A saved snapshot is taken on purpose with Save snapshot…, under a title and a note, for example Kickoff review. Nothing later ever replaces it.

What a record does not hold matters just as much:

  • The steps themselves. It keeps counts, not a list. Nobody can tell later which step was done that day, or when a step finished.
  • The graph.
  • Who was working. The team and the focus of that day are baked into its dates. A record cannot be recomputed for a different team.

2. Picking then and now

The strip reads Compare [then] with [now]. Each side is one of the following:

  • Plan at start (the then side's default). This is the last record on or before the project's start date. If the project is older than its history, it is the first record there is. It is never today's own record, because that is the plan now, and comparing it with itself would compare nothing.
  • Day… is the last record on or before the day you pick. If no record was written that day, an earlier one stands in, and the heading says so.
  • A saved snapshot, found by its title.
  • Now (the now side's default) is the live plan, worked out fresh. It is not a record at all. If you pick a record as now instead, every curve stops on that record's day.

Every heading names the two plans it compares, including the day of the record that stood in, for example “versus the plan at 8 September, recorded 7 September”. A comparison should never leave you guessing what it was measured against.

3. Reading the plots

All the plots share one date axis, so a day sits at the same place in every one of them. The solid vertical line is today, and a dashed vertical line marks a saved snapshot.

Progress, and Scope change

Progress puts two lines side by side:

  • The plan now, drawn in each milestone's colour, is the share of all estimated days the plan says should have landed by each date.
  • What actually landed, drawn in ink, is one point per recorded day.

The words beside today's dot (ahead 5%, behind 12%) are the gap between the two lines today.

Scope change puts the plan then (dashed) over the plan now. The area between them is coloured by which way the plan moved:

  • Red where the plan now promises less work done by a date than it did then: the plan has slipped.
  • Amber where it promises more: work has been pulled in.
  • A green line where the two agree.

In the example, adding Fix the import on Thursday turns the end of the plan red.

The small example already shows three things to read with care:

  • The actual line drops on Thursday, though nothing was undone. Adding a step made the total bigger, so the same done work became a smaller share (section 4).
  • Friday reads behind 12%, though Polish is exactly on schedule. The plan line rises straight through the three days Polish takes, but Polish only counts once it is done (section 5, P1).
  • The actual line starts above zero. Monday's record is Monday's last state, with Design already done. So “the plan at start” is the plan as it stood at the end of its first day.

Milestone shifts

The same comparison, as the Time tab words it:

Volume

  • Scope volume is the total of estimated days the plan came to on each recorded day.
  • Remaining work is that total less what had landed.

Both are steps rather than slopes, because a record says what the plan was until the next one.

4. Why the shares can mislead, and why Volume exists

Progress and Scope change both draw shares, and each share is of that day's own total. So a plan that doubled overnight, with half of it done, reads 50% on both days:

The share never moved, but the work left doubled. The Volume page is the only place that shows it.

A share measured against a growing total also means the actual line of earlier days was measured against a smaller plan than today's. When scope creeps in, the actual line can sit above the plan now for weeks.

5. Where a comparison can lie

The prototype found these by playing scenarios and replaying real plans. ISSUES.md has the evidence for each; the ids here point into it.

  • The landing dates never learn from what has landed (F1). The forecast re-plays the whole plan from its start date every day, as if every step took exactly its estimate.
    • Work that runs late shows up only as a gap on the Progress plot, never in the dates.
    • A milestone can go on promising a date that has already passed.
  • “Behind” while exactly on time (P1). The plan line is drawn straight between the days steps land, but a step only counts as done when it lands.
    • While a multi-day step is under way, the plan line pulls ahead of the actual line.
    • In the example, Polish is on schedule on Friday, yet the plot reads behind.
  • A plan with no start date is compared with itself (F2). Its start is “today”, every day. So every landing slides by a day each day, and Plan at start resolves to today's own record.
  • Scope change also counts other changes as scope (R2). A person joining, a new focus factor or a moved start date all move the plan now against a record that froze its day's team. The colours read “pulled in” or “slipped” with no scope changed at all.
  • Days with no record are drawn as straight lines (R1). The actual line runs straight between the recorded days. A week the window stayed closed is a guess drawn as a line.
